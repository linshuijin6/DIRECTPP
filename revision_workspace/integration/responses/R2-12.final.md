We thank the reviewer for emphasizing the importance of direct statistical comparisons when evaluating classification performance. We agree that differences in point estimates alone are insufficient to fully characterize performance differences between methods. Accordingly, we conducted paired analyses using predictions from the same held-out subjects. Stratified paired bootstrap resampling was used to estimate performance differences and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and fold-wise paired DeLong tests were performed for AUC comparisons, with Holm correction for multiple comparisons.

Specifically, for AD versus CN classification, combining MRI with synthetic dual-tracer PET improved AUC by 6.80 percentage points (95% CI: 3.39–10.22) and balanced accuracy by 9.06 percentage points (95% CI: 4.68–13.46) compared with MRI alone. For MCI versus CN classification, the AUC difference was −2.13 percentage points (95% CI: −6.30 to 2.10), without a clear improvement. For the more fine-grained EMCI versus LMCI classification task, AUC increased by 3.52 percentage points (95% CI: −2.12 to 9.50), while balanced accuracy improved by 6.91 percentage points (95% CI: 2.02–11.98). The fold-wise DeLong comparisons did not reach statistical significance after Holm correction.

The differences across classification tasks may be related to the underlying disease stages and their imaging characteristics. AD and CN represent relatively distinct clinical states, often accompanied by more pronounced group-level differences in cerebral metabolism and amyloid deposition, making their discrimination comparatively straightforward. In contrast, MCI is clinically and pathologically heterogeneous, with imaging characteristics that may overlap with those of cognitively normal individuals and patients with AD. Distinguishing EMCI from LMCI is particularly challenging because the differences in disease-related imaging features between these stages may be relatively subtle.

Notably, even in the challenging EMCI versus LMCI task, incorporating synthetic PET yielded higher balanced accuracy, suggesting that the synthesized images may provide useful information for fine-grained MCI stage discrimination. In the revised manuscript, we will present these findings alongside the corresponding confidence intervals and statistical comparisons, and refine the original statements regarding statistical superiority to provide a more accurate interpretation of the contribution of synthetic PET to downstream classification.
