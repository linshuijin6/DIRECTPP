We thank the reviewer for these detailed and constructive suggestions regarding image-quality evaluation, statistical analysis, and visualization. We agree that normalized radar plots alone are insufficient to convey the absolute magnitude, inter-subject variability, and spatial distribution of reconstruction errors. Accordingly, we have strengthened the quantitative evaluation and provided additional statistical and visual evidence.

First, regarding absolute image-quality metrics and Figure 2, the normalized radar plots were originally intended to facilitate comparisons across SSIM, PSNR, MSE, and MAE, which have different numerical ranges and optimization directions. We recognize that this presentation does not adequately communicate the absolute performance of each method. We have therefore retained Figure 2 and added a quantitative table to the main manuscript reporting the absolute values of all four metrics for the compared methods on ADNI and OASIS.

We also revisited the original experimental records to assess the availability of subject-level statistics. Although the final aggregate metrics underlying Figure 2 were preserved, some historical subject-level evaluation records and corresponding data-partition information could not be fully recovered. Consequently, it would not be reliable to retrospectively derive standard deviations or paired significance tests for every historical baseline. Given the substantial computational cost of retraining all historical methods, we focused our additional validation on representative key comparisons under a common, fixed two-fold subject-level partition.

Specifically, we re-executed DIReCT++ and evaluated it alongside a clinically conditioned regression 3D U-Net, Swin-UNETR, single-tracer DIReCT, a tabular-MLP conditioning baseline, and a tracer-only conditioning baseline. These experiments retained subject-level evaluation records for 1,598 FDG and 1,365 AV45 PET observations. In the Supplementary Materials, we provide the mean ± standard deviation of SSIM, PSNR, MSE, and MAE, together with paired performance differences, 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, effect sizes, and Holm-adjusted statistical tests. We also clarify the conditioning information and training configurations of the compared methods.

In these comparisons, DIReCT++ achieved improvements over the clinically matched regression 3D U-Net across all four whole-volume metrics for both tracers, supported by paired statistical analyses. Comparisons with the other baselines showed metric-dependent outcomes rather than uniform superiority. We have therefore moderated the corresponding conclusions and avoided attributing differences between independently trained models to individual architectural components without sufficient experimental control. We also distinguish the original multi-method comparison from the subject-matched evaluation rather than combining their results in a single statistical analysis.

Second, regarding brain-mask-based evaluation, we agree that background voxels can influence whole-volume image-quality metrics. We therefore additionally calculated SSIM, PSNR, MSE, and MAE within a predefined mask derived from the frozen SynthSeg segmentation labels. The mask includes cortical and subcortical structures, white matter, and other intracranial regions, including ventricular and cerebrospinal fluid compartments. Its anatomical coverage and the valid-window procedure used for masked SSIM are explicitly described in the Supplementary Materials.

After applying the predefined quality-control exclusion, this evaluation included 1,597 FDG and 1,364 AV45 PET observations. DIReCT++ achieved better mean values for all four masked metrics than both the clinically conditioned regression 3D U-Net and Swin-UNETR for both tracers. All 16 paired comparisons reached statistical significance after Holm correction within their respective predefined testing families. Nevertheless, some standardized effect sizes were small, particularly for AV45 error metrics. We therefore distinguish statistical significance from the magnitude and potential practical relevance of the observed improvements. The complete masked metrics, inter-subject standard deviations, confidence intervals, and paired statistical results are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Third, regarding case selection and voxel-wise error visualization, the examples in Figures 2 and 3 were randomly selected within their respective diagnostic groups to illustrate qualitative PET synthesis results across different clinical categories. We have clarified this selection criterion in the figure captions.

To provide an additional objective assessment, we have prepared supplementary examples using a predefined, performance-based selection procedure. For each tracer, cases were selected according to subject-level SSIM from the fixed two-fold evaluation, including examples near the median and 10th percentile. The corresponding visualizations show the input MRI, real PET, synthetic PET, and voxel-wise absolute error maps.

We also examined the lowest-SSIM cases and identified a previously documented MRI/segmentation quality-control issue affecting the lowest-ranked subject. We distinguish this data-quality issue from reconstruction errors attributable to the model and do not interpret this case as representative of typical model failure.

Fourth, regarding OASIS evaluation and ROC visualization, we clarify that the external evaluation on OASIS was restricted to AV45 PET. The original absolute evaluation metrics are now presented in the main manuscript, and the interpretation of external generalizability is limited to the evaluated tracer and cohort. These historical results are not combined with the new subject-matched ADNI experiments for paired statistical inference.

For downstream classification, we additionally provide ROC curves with 95% bootstrap confidence bands for the AD versus CN, MCI versus CN, and EMCI versus LMCI tasks, based on the predictions from the three-fold classification evaluation. These supplementary figures illustrate the performance and statistical uncertainty of the evaluated input configurations.

Finally, regarding composite SUVR scatter plots and amyloid-positivity analysis, we acknowledge the clinical relevance of these assessments. However, the PET intensities in the present study underwent per-image min–max normalization, and the resulting regional uptake measurements are not clinically calibrated SUVR or Centiloid values. Applying conventional clinical SUVR thresholds or deriving amyloid-positivity classifications directly from these normalized intensities would therefore risk producing quantitatively unsupported conclusions. We have clarified this limitation and identify standardized PET quantification and amyloid-status agreement as directions for future investigation.

Overall, we have retained the original multi-method visualization while adding absolute quantitative results to the main manuscript and incorporating subject-level statistics, brain-mask-based evaluation, paired comparisons, error visualizations, and ROC confidence bands into the Supplementary Materials. These additions provide a more transparent and reproducible assessment of reconstruction quality while ensuring that the interpretation remains consistent with the available evidence.
