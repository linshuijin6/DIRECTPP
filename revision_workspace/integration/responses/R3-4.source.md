We thank the reviewer for emphasizing the importance of subject-level PET uptake agreement and amyloid-positivity assessment. We agree that, beyond evaluating the preservation of disease-related uptake patterns, quantitative agreement at the individual-subject level is an important aspect of assessing synthetic PET image quality.

In the original study, we used paired regional uptake comparisons and AD-versus-CN group analyses to evaluate the agreement between synthetic and real PET images and to examine whether the synthesized images retained disease-related regional uptake differences. These analyses provided an initial assessment of regional uptake characteristics. However, we agree that the absence of statistically significant mean differences in paired tests alone does not fully characterize subject-level agreement.

To provide a more comprehensive evaluation, we extended our analysis using the preserved subject-matched regional uptake data for both AV45 and FDG PET. Across all 98 regions of interest (ROIs), we calculated Lin's concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), mean absolute error (MAE), Bland–Altman bias and 95% limits of agreement, together with mean paired differences and their 95% confidence intervals. These analyses were performed for the overall cohorts and separately within the diagnostic subgroups, allowing us to examine regional agreement, subject-level paired errors, and systematic differences from complementary perspectives.

Across the 98 ROIs in the overall cohorts, the median CCC values were 0.9745 for AV45 and 0.9450 for FDG, while the corresponding median MAEs were 0.0160 and 0.0159, respectively, in normalized intensity units. These results indicate generally strong agreement in regional uptake between synthetic and real PET, while also revealing variations in individual-level errors across brain regions. We will incorporate these additional agreement measures into the revised manuscript and refine the interpretation of the original paired-test results accordingly.

We also appreciate the reviewer's suggestion to evaluate formal equivalence and amyloid-positivity agreement, particularly in amyloid-positive cognitively normal individuals and amyloid-negative patients with MCI. These assessments are highly relevant to the clinical application of synthetic PET. Our present study focuses on MRI-to-PET image synthesis and the reconstruction of regional uptake characteristics. The PET images were processed using per-image min–max intensity normalization, so the resulting regional measurements represent normalized uptake rather than clinically calibrated SUVR or Centiloid values. Consequently, standard amyloid-positivity thresholds cannot be directly applied to these measurements, while formal equivalence testing would require clinically justified equivalence margins.

We will clarify this quantitative scope in the revised manuscript. Future work incorporating PET data with standardized quantitative calibration will enable further evaluation of SUVR or Centiloid agreement and amyloid-positivity classification, including the clinically important atypical cases highlighted by the reviewer.
