1. Precomputed tracer conditioning versus learnable tracer tokens

We appreciate the reviewer’s observation regarding the representation of tracer-specific conditioning information. We agree that the precomputed tracer embeddings are used as fixed tracer-specific tokens during generator training and inference. However, we would like to emphasize that keeping these conditioning vectors fixed during generation does not make their information sources equivalent to those of randomly initialized learnable tokens.

DIReCT++ employs a pre-trained BiomedCLIP model to encode biomedical text descriptions of FDG and AV45. These descriptions not only identify the tracer modalities but also incorporate relevant biomedical information. For example, the FDG description includes cerebral glucose metabolism, neuronal energy demands, and metabolic alterations associated with neurodegenerative diseases. In contrast, the AV45 description characterizes amyloid-beta deposition and its spatial distribution across relevant brain regions. By encoding these descriptions into tracer-specific conditioning vectors, our approach aims to leverage the semantic representations acquired through biomedical image-text pretraining to provide medically informed conditioning for different PET modalities.

Compared with learning tracer embeddings directly from random initialization, the principal distinction of this design lies in the origin of the conditioning representations. The tracer features are constructed from text descriptions with explicit biomedical meanings, rather than being determined solely by trainable parameters optimized for the current generation task. Furthermore, the lightweight affine adaptation mechanism is designed to adjust these tracer-text representations using corresponding PET image features, while a cross-tracer similarity constraint regulates the representational relationship between the two tracers.

Therefore, we consider the primary role of BiomedCLIP in this framework to be the provision of biomedical semantic representations for tracer conditioning and a unified text-based conditioning mechanism for multi-tracer PET synthesis. In the revised manuscript, we will describe this mechanism more precisely, emphasizing the construction and adaptation of pre-trained semantic features rather than suggesting continuous end-to-end updating of the vision-language model during PET generation.

1. Numerical clinical encoding and comparison with TabularMLP

We understand the reviewer’s concern regarding the ability of CLIP-based text encoders to represent numerical clinical variables. However, we would like to clarify that the purpose of clinical text conditioning in our study is not merely to transform numerical values such as age, MMSE, and CDR into high-dimensional vectors. Instead, our approach incorporates both the numerical measurements and their corresponding medical interpretations into the conditioning representation.

Specifically, the patient-specific clinical prompts in DIReCT++ contain eight variables: age, sex, weight, CDR, MMSE, GDS, FAQ, and NPI-Q. Importantly, these prompts also include descriptions of the clinical meaning and scoring ranges of the corresponding assessments. For example, MMSE is explicitly described as an assessment of cognitive function, together with its scoring range and interpretation, whereas CDR is associated with the clinical assessment of dementia severity. The remaining clinical assessments are similarly accompanied by descriptions of the functions or symptoms they evaluate.

Through this prompt construction strategy, clinical conditioning is represented not simply as a collection of isolated numerical values, but as structured natural-language descriptions combining patient-specific measurements with relevant medical context. The pre-trained BiomedCLIP text encoder then maps these descriptions into a shared biomedical semantic feature space.

In contrast, TabularMLP directly learns feature mappings from structured clinical variables without incorporating additional natural-language descriptions. Therefore, the distinction between the two approaches extends beyond their network architectures to the representation of clinical information and the prior information used during encoding.

We further investigated whether patient-specific clinical conditioning influences the actual generation process. Our implementation-level analyses confirmed that clinical prompts from different patients produce distinct feature vectors and that these conditioning features participate in generation through cross-attention, producing measurable changes in the synthesized outputs.

To further assess different clinical encoding strategies, we conducted additional experiments using a TabularMLP-based conditioning model. This model processes the same eight structured clinical variables through a trainable multilayer perceptron to produce a 512-dimensional patient-conditioning vector, while retaining the tracer conditioning and the overall generative framework.

The experimental results revealed metric-dependent trade-offs between BiomedCLIP-based text encoding and TabularMLP-based numerical encoding. The full model achieved higher SSIM for both FDG and AV45, whereas TabularMLP showed advantages in certain error-related metrics. These findings suggest that different conditioning representations may influence structural image similarity and voxel-wise reconstruction errors differently.

We believe that the research value of BiomedCLIP-based text encoding lies in its integration of pre-trained biomedical semantic representations with clinical measurements and their medical interpretations, allowing patient-specific information to participate in PET generation within a clinically contextualized representation space. Compared with relying exclusively on numerical feature learning, this provides an alternative conditioning strategy with a clear methodological motivation.

Furthermore, conventional image-quality metrics, including SSIM, PSNR, MSE, and MAE, primarily assess overall image similarity and reconstruction errors. In contrast, clinical information related to disease status and cognitive function may be more closely associated with regional metabolic distributions and disease-related tracer uptake patterns. Therefore, the value of different encoding strategies should also be evaluated in relation to their effects on clinically relevant imaging characteristics. In future work, we plan to incorporate regional SUV/SUVR-based quantitative analyses and more detailed clinical evaluations to investigate the potential contribution of biomedical semantic conditioning to functional image reconstruction.

1. Three-dimensional PET feature extraction and cross-tracer similarity constraint

Regarding the application of the 2D BiomedCLIP model to 3D PET images, we clarify that, in the affine-adapter feature-extraction procedure, each preprocessed PET volume is represented by 160 two-dimensional slices. Each slice undergoes intensity normalization, resizing, and conversion to a three-channel image before being processed by the frozen BiomedCLIP image encoder to obtain a 512-dimensional feature vector. The slice-level features are subsequently aggregated through average pooling to form a subject-level PET representation. The revised implementation applies the same feature-extraction procedure to both FDG and AV45.

Regarding the cross-tracer similarity constraint, we agree that the one-sided inequality presented in the original manuscript does not accurately represent the implemented optimization objective. The adapter optimization employs a two-sided squared soft penalty with a target similarity of τ, rather than simply encouraging the similarity between the two tracer representations to increase continuously.

Specifically, τ = 0.82 corresponds to the two-decimal approximation of the cosine similarity between the original FDG and AV45 BiomedCLIP text embeddings before adaptation (0.8205). We further conducted representation-level analyses using different τ settings and a configuration in which the cross-tracer similarity penalty was removed. The results demonstrate that this constraint regulates the similarity between tracer representations and produces an observable trade-off between text-image feature alignment and inter-tracer representational separation.

Thus, the purpose of this mechanism is to regulate the relative relationship between the adapted representations, rather than simply maximizing their similarity. We will clarify the implemented optimization objective in the revised manuscript and accurately describe the representation-level characteristics demonstrated by the corresponding experiments.

1. Overall clarification

Taken together, we consider the central role of the vision-language model in DIReCT++ to be the provision of biomedical semantic representations for tracer knowledge and patient-specific clinical information, supporting multi-tracer PET synthesis through a shared conditioning mechanism.

Compared with conditioning approaches based solely on randomly initialized vectors or directly encoded structured numerical variables, BiomedCLIP-based text conditioning offers a distinct representation strategy. On the one hand, tracer conditioning originates from text descriptions containing explicit biomedical knowledge. On the other hand, the patient-specific clinical prompts incorporate numerical measurements together with the medical meanings of the corresponding assessments, allowing clinical information to participate in generation within a shared biomedical text representation space.

Our additional analyses demonstrate that patient-specific conditioning influences the generated outputs, that BiomedCLIP-based and TabularMLP-based encoding strategies exhibit trade-offs across different image-quality metrics, and that the tracer adaptation mechanism regulates the relative relationship between tracer representations at the feature level. These findings provide further insight into the roles of different conditioning representations within the PET synthesis framework.
