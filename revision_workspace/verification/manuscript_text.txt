17                                                  Abstract

18       Objective: To evaluate DIReCT++, a domain-informed rectified flow framework for synthe-
19       sizing FDG- and AV45-PET from MRI and clinical text inputs.
20       Impact Statement: This work evaluates multi-tracer synthesis and cross-sectional MCI stage
21       discrimination, with explicit limits on clinical and prognostic interpretation.
22       Introduction: PET cost, radiation exposure and limited accessibility motivate MRI-based
23       synthesis, but image similarity alone does not establish individual molecular validity.
24       Methods: A shared 3D rectified flow generator uses frozen BiomedCLIP-derived patient and
25       tracer features for learned one-step inference. Separate analyses evaluate affine tracer adap-
26       tation, two-fold OOF generation, normalized regional uptake agreement and three-fold cross-
27       sectional classification. ADNI includes 1,857 participants; historical OASIS-3 external evalua-
28       tion is restricted to AV45 in CN/AD.
29       Results: Masked OOF comparisons favored Full over a clinical regression U-Net and MRI-only
30       Swin-UNETR in all 16 predefined comparisons within their respective Holm families, although
31       some effects were small. Other conditioning comparisons showed metric-dependent tradeoffs.
32       In AD/CN classification, MRI+Syn-both AUC was 93.82 ± 3.21% versus MRI-only 87.02 ±


                                                         1
33         5.44%; MCI/CN did not improve, and EMCI/LMCI showed mixed endpoints. Clinical-only
34         and MRI+Clinical AUC exceeded MRI+Syn-both. Historical regional intensities showed close
35         agreement, without establishing clinical SUV/SUVR calibration or equivalence.
36         Conclusion: DIReCT++ supports further investigation of multi-tracer synthesis and cross-
37         sectional classification. Extensive diagnosis-conditioned filling of clinical scores, source-identity
38         limitations and mixed task effects preclude claims of independent diagnostic value, longitudinal
39         prognosis or replacement of molecular confirmation.

42   1     Introduction
43   Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, constitutes a mounting
44   global health crisis. Its pathological processes begin years before clinical dementia manifests, making
45   the prodromal stage of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) a critical window for intervention [1].
46   However, the heterogeneous nature of MCI, where only a subset of individuals progress to AD,
47   poses a significant prognostic challenge [2]. This challenge is underscored by the modern biological
48   definition of AD, which is based on in vivo evidence of core pathologies, specifically amyloid-β (Aβ)
                            18                                                 18
49   deposition (e.g., on        F-AV45 PET) and neuronal injury (e.g.,             F-FDG PET hypometabolism),
50   with structural MRI quantifying associated atrophy [2–4]. In clinical practice, however, the routine
51   acquisition of PET is hampered by high cost, limited access, and radiation exposure, restricting this
52   multi-modal biomarker profiling to research settings and hindering timely, widespread screening [5].
53       Generative artificial intelligence (AI) offers a transformative pathway to bridge this clinical gap
54   by synthesizing PET biomarkers from routinely acquired MRI [6, 7]. Initial studies, primarily using
55   generative adversarial networks (GANs) [8–10] and, more recently, diffusion models [11–13], have
56   established the feasibility of producing visually realistic PET images. Subsequent advances have
57   sought to improve fidelity by incorporating domain knowledge, for instance, through guidance from
58   large vision-language models (VLMs) [14, 15]. Despite these advances, a critical limitation remains:
59   the subject-specific precision necessary for cross-sectional diagnosis and MCI stage discrimination is
60   still lacking, which is particularly crucial for a heterogeneous condition like AD [3]. This challenge
61   stems from the inherent nature of MRI-to-PET synthesis as an ill-posed inverse problem; neurode-
62   generation visible on MRI is typically a downstream effect of prior molecular pathologies captured
63   by PET [16]. Consequently, individuals with similar structural MRI presentations at early disease
64   stages can exhibit vastly different pathological burdens, leading to ambiguous mapping. Overcom-
65   ing this ambiguity requires an AI synthesis framework that can comprehensively model complex
66   cross-modal and cross-tracer associations to provide strong, patient-specific guidance.
67       Building upon single-tracer DIReCT [15], DIReCT++ (Domain-Informed ReCTified flow) ex-
68   tends 3D rectified flow to a shared network conditioned on the target PET tracer, enabling FDG- or
69   AV45-PET generation in separate forward passes. A second extension is lightweight affine adaptation
70   of BiomedCLIP [17]-derived tracer features using text–PET alignment and a cross-tracer soft similar-
71   ity target. We distinguish this adaptation formulation from reconstructed generator configurations


                                                            2
 72   using raw frozen tracer vectors. The framework combines tracer knowledge with patient-specific clin-
 73   ical text and learned one-step inference. Its components are evaluated with configuration-specific
 74   comparisons rather than assumed to resolve the biological ambiguity of MRI-to-PET translation.
 75       We evaluate image synthesis on ADNI [18] and retain historical AV45 external results from
 76   OASIS-3 [19]. Regional analyses examine normalized uptake agreement and AD–CN effect patterns.
 77   Separate DenseNet-based classification [20] evaluates AD/CN, MCI/CN and EMCI/LMCI under
 78   fixed three-fold splits, alongside Clinical-only and MRI+Clinical baselines. The study addresses
 79   cross-sectional performance and methodological tradeoffs; it does not test future AD conversion,
 80   establish clinical equivalence or validate synthetic PET as molecular confirmation.


 81   2     Results
 82   2.1     The DIReCT++ Framework for Precision PET Synthesis
 83   DIReCT++ is a VLM-modulated rectified flow framework that synthesizes FDG- or AV45-PET
 84   estimates from MRI with patient and tracer text guidance (Fig. 1A). Its conditional 3D U-Net
 85   predicts a tracer-specific velocity field for learned one-step inference (Fig. 1C). The shared network
 86   handles both tracer targets; this does not imply simultaneous dual-tracer output or clinical molecular
 87   validity.
 88       Text guidance incorporates tracer descriptions and patient information (age, sex, weight and
 89   clinical scores) encoded by frozen BiomedCLIP encoders and injected into the RF model through
 90   cross-attention. The affine fine-tuning formulation optimizes lightweight adapters of BiomedCLIP-
 91   derived tracer guidance, rather than BiomedCLIP encoder weights, using a soft cross-tracer similarity
 92   penalty (Fig. 1B). In a separate adapter-level analysis, varying τ from 0.70 to 0.98 changed the mean
 93   cross-tracer embedding similarity from 0.7032 to 0.9807. At τ = 0.82, the similarity was 0.8214,
 94   compared with 0.9897 without the cross-tracer penalty (Table S6). These are representation-level
 95   results, not a comparison of PET generation quality across τ . The reconstructed Full generation
 96   experiments used the original fixed tracer embeddings; their performance should not be attributed
 97   to affine adaptation.
 98       A separate E1 fixed-weight sensitivity analysis replaced the clinical-score portion of the patient
 99   prompt while retaining age, sex and weight, MRI, tracer and model parameters (Table S17). Re-
100   moving scores increased ROI MAE by approximately 0.000036 for FDG and 0.000079 for AV45,
101   with statistical support under the endpoint-specific two-tracer Holm families but very small abso-
102   lute changes. SSIM increased while PSNR decreased, and the average absolute prediction change
103   was approximately 0.001019/0.001105 for FDG/AV45. These observations show conditional output
104   sensitivity; they are not a retrained No-Scores ablation or proof of independent diagnostic benefit.
105       At inference, the shared network predicts a velocity field for the selected tracer from MRI and
106   the corresponding two-token text context. A single forward pass produces one tracer-specific PET
107   estimate; FDG and AV45 are obtained in separate tracer-conditioned passes (Fig. 1D). The resulting
108   images are evaluated through image-quality measures, regional uptake analysis and cross-sectional
109   classification (Fig. 1E).

110   2.2    PET Synthesis Quality and External Evaluation
111   PET synthesis was evaluated using structural similarity (SSIM), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR),
112   mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). Figure 2 retains the historical com-
113   parisons with CycleGAN [21], Swin-UNet [22], segmentation-guided diffusion [23], 3D DDIM [24,
114   25], 3D rectified flow [26] and single-tracer DIReCT [15]. Absolute historical aggregate values are
115   given in Table 1; subject-level statistics were not recoverable for every historical method and are
116   not reconstructed from these aggregates. PET synthesis used two-fold ADNI evaluation; histori-
117   cal external evaluation used OASIS-3 AV45 only. The reconstructed OOF comparisons and their
118   statistical protocols are reported separately from the historical Figure 2 summaries.
119       The reconstructed OOF evaluation does not support a uniform advantage of Full over all alter-
120   natives (Tables S9,S10). Full outperformed the clinically conditioned regression U-Net across the
121   four whole-volume metrics for both tracers, while the magnitude of the AV45 MSE effect was small
122   (|dz | = 0.075). Relative to TabularMLP, Full had higher SSIM but lower PSNR and higher MSE for
123   both tracers; MAE did not differ after the specified Holm correction. TracerOnly had better FDG
124   results on all four metrics and higher AV45 SSIM than Full. Single-tracer DIReCT had higher FDG
125   SSIM, whereas Full had better FDG PSNR/MSE and AV45 SSIM. These independently trained
126   models differ in initialization, conditioning or implementation, so the comparisons do not isolate a
127   single causal component or prove multi-tracer synergy.
128      In the masked evaluation (Tables S11,S12), Full had higher mean SSIM/PSNR and lower mean
129   MSE/MAE than Clinical U-Net and MRI-only Swin-UNETR for both tracers; all 16 predefined
130   comparisons met pHolm < 0.05 within their two separate eight-test families. The masked cohort
131   contained 1,597 FDG and 1,364 AV45 observations after the existing MRI/mask QC exclusion. For
132   Clinical U-Net AV45 MSE/MAE, the standardized effects were small (|dz | = 0.071/0.076), and only
133   56.4–57.8% of subjects favored Full. Thus statistical support does not imply uniform individual or
134   clinical benefit. Full/Clinical U-Net used epoch 165, while MRI-only Swin-UNETR used epoch 200;
135   information and training endpoints differ.
136      Separate whole-volume and masked summaries are retained because background exclusion
137   changes the metric definitions and values. Supplemental FDG and AV45 examples use the median,
138   10th-percentile and worst QC-passed subjects selected by saved whole-volume SSIM, and show
139   MRI, real PET, synthetic PET and absolute-error maps (Figs. S7,S8).
140      The historical OASIS-3 evaluation reports AV45 PSNR of 26.79 dB, compared with 28.09 dB for
141   historical internal ADNI AV45 evaluation (Fig. 2A; Table 1). The cross-dataset examples in Fig. 2C
142   illustrate CN/AD AV45 synthesis. These aggregate and illustrative results do not provide subject-
143   level confidence intervals, FDG external evidence or external MCI-stage validation, and should not
144   be combined with the reconstructed OOF statistics as a single experiment.

147   2.3      Recapitulation of Disease-Specific Biomarker Patterns
148   To assess regional uptake patterns, the corresponding T1-weighted MRI scans were parcellated using
149   SynthSeg [27], and the resulting Desikan–Killiany and aseg ROI masks were applied to spatially
150   aligned real and synthetic PET images. This approach yielded a total of 98 distinct regions of interest
151   (ROIs), including cortical, subcortical, cerebellar, and brainstem structures. Regional normalized
152   uptake values (arbitrary units) were computed as the mean voxel intensity within each ROI from
153   PET images normalized using per-image min–max intensity scaling. Fig. 3 illustrates this analysis
154   for key ROIs in two representative ADNI subjects, illustrating regional patterns in these selected
155   cases.
156      For a comprehensive quantitative evaluation, we performed two complementary regional sta-
157   tistical analyses on the ADNI cohort. First, we compared synthetic and real images to assess the
158   normalized regional uptake agreement of DIReCT++. Second, we compared the AD and CN groups
159   separately in real and synthetic PET to assess whether disease-related regional differences were re-
160   tained. Figure 4A,B uses the pooled available subjects without diagnostic-group stratification; panels
161   C–F compare AD and CN only.
162      Paired-sample t-tests between synthetic and real images revealed no statistically significant dif-
163   ferences in normalized regional uptake across the principal brain regions shown in Fig. 4A, B for
164   the overall cohorts of both tracers (p > 0.05). Across all 98 ROIs, no overall comparisons re-
165   mained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction (all q > 0.05).

229   3     Discussion
230   DIReCT++ combines shared tracer-conditioned 3D rectified flow with biomedical text guidance
231   for FDG- and AV45-PET synthesis from MRI. Its contribution extends the single-tracer DIReCT
232   framework to multi-tracer modeling and a separately evaluated affine-adapter formulation. The
233   evidence comprises historical aggregate comparisons, reconstructed OOF generation, normalized
234   regional uptake statistics and a separate cross-sectional classification analysis. These sources should
235   be interpreted within their respective input and execution identities rather than as a single verified
236   historical run.
237       The generation results show both advantages and tradeoffs. Full had better masked image-
238   quality means than the clinical regression U-Net and MRI-only Swin-UNETR, while some AV45
239   error effects were small and did not imply universal individual benefit. Whole-volume comparisons
240   favored TabularMLP for PSNR/MSE and TracerOnly for several endpoints. The adapter analysis
241   supports control of tracer-embedding geometry, but it does not establish a causal improvement in
242   PET generation. Fixed-weight conditioning interventions indicate that patient features can influence
243   outputs; independently initialized Full/TracerOnly or text/tabular models do not isolate the clinical
244   or VLM component as the sole cause of their performance differences.

245      The potential value of this framework is to improve access to biomarker-related imaging infor-
246   mation for multimodal imaging research. The classification findings are task dependent: MRI+Syn-
247   both improved AD/CN relative to MRI, did not improve MCI/CN, and showed mixed EMCI/LMCI
248   endpoints. Clinical-only and MRI+Clinical AUC exceeded MRI+Syn-both in the completed compar-
249   isons. Because clinical inputs include diagnosis-related assessments and diagnosis-conditioned fills,
250   these findings cannot be used to establish independent diagnostic value of synthetic PET. Similar
251   observed Real/Syn performance does not demonstrate equivalence or molecular validity.
252      Synthetic PET may support exploratory MRI-based analyses when PET is unavailable, but
253   clinical screening or treatment selection would require independent molecular biomarker confirmation
254   and prospective validation. No longitudinal conversion, progressive-versus-stable MCI, survival or
255   treatment-response endpoints were analyzed. EMCI/LMCI classification is therefore MCI stage
256   discrimination; it does not predict an individual’s progression to AD. Whether serially synthesized
257   PET can reflect progression or treatment response remains a question for longitudinal studies.
258      Within medical image synthesis, DIReCT++ evaluates disease-related regional uptake patterns
259   and downstream classification in addition to image-level metrics. The shared flow model and biomed-
260   ical conditioning provide a framework for further study, rather than establishing clinically actionable
261   biomarkers or attributing all overall differences to one architectural component. The learned one-
262   step approximation permits a single tracer-conditioned forward pass; measured forward latency does
263   not constitute an end-to-end workflow or an equal-protocol speedup over competing models.
264      Several limitations require explicit consideration. Clinical-record reconstruction showed extensive
265   diagnosis-conditioned filling of missing scores, which can introduce group information into condi-
266   tioning and classification. Source weight entries also contain unresolved validity issues. The original
267   random filling states and complete actual acquisition mappings were not recovered. The independent
268   three-fold classifier evaluation verifies its own partitions, but the historical synthetic-image inputs
269   could not be bound directly to the checkpoints in the reconstructed generation comparisons. These
270   limitations constrain claims of reproducibility and independent clinical discrimination.
271      Regional measurements use per-image min–max-normalized intensities in arbitrary units, with-
272   out established anatomical-reference SUV/SUVR calibration. No clinically justified equivalence
273   margin, formal TOST result or valid amyloid-positivity agreement analysis was established. Close
274   agreement, non-significant paired differences or preservation of AD–CN significance is not evidence
275   of clinical equivalence or reliable atypical Aβ status classification. Calibrated quantitative PET and
276   prespecified, clinically meaningful equivalence margins are needed for such assessments.
277      The deterministic one-step formulation provides a single output per input without calibrated
278   subject-level predictive uncertainty. An MSE-trained deterministic model may represent an average
279   conditional mapping and cannot guarantee recovery of individual molecular pathology from MRI and
280   text. Ensemble or stochastic uncertainty estimation remains future work. Not all requested single-
281   component ablations or matched one-step/multistep evaluations were completed. The fixed-model
282   bootstrap intervals do not quantify training-seed or split variability.
283      External evaluation was limited to historical AV45 synthesis in OASIS-3 CN/AD, with age dif-
284   ferences and incomplete run-specific prompt records; FDG and MCI external generalizability remain
285   unestablished. Prospective validation in more diverse clinical populations, including alternative MRI


                                                        13
286   acquisitions [28], is needed. Additional clinical modalities such as blood biomarkers [29] may pro-
287   vide complementary information, but their incremental value must be evaluated independently. The
288   2024 Alzheimer’s Association criteria emphasize biological diagnosis using validated core biomarkers,
289   including amyloid PET and sufficiently accurate blood-based assays such as plasma p-tau217 [30].
290   Synthetic PET has not been validated as a substitute for molecular confirmation, and its added
291   value relative to those biomarker assessments remains to be determined.
292       In conclusion, DIReCT++ provides a shared tracer-conditioned framework for PET synthesis
293   with biomedical text inputs and learned one-step inference. The reported image-quality and regional
294   results support further methodological investigation, while mixed classification effects, clinical-input
295   circularity and unresolved source limitations constrain clinical interpretation. Longitudinal prog-
296   nosis, clinical equivalence and replacement of molecular biomarker confirmation require separate
297   validation.


298   4     Materials and Methods
299   4.1    Experimental Design
300   The clinical management of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is limited by the practical challenges associated
301   with routine multi-tracer positron emission tomography (PET), including acquisition cost, radiation
302   exposure, and accessibility. This study was designed to investigate whether molecular PET images
303   associated with Alzheimer’s disease pathology could be synthesized from a single structural MRI
304   scan while preserving biologically and clinically relevant information.
305       We developed DIReCT++, an MRI-conditioned generative framework for subject-specific syn-
306   thesis of amyloid and FDG PET images. The framework was designed to address the inherently
307   ill-posed nature of MRI-to-PET translation by incorporating patient-specific clinical information
308   and tracer-related knowledge into the generation process.
309       The study was evaluated using a multi-level validation strategy. Paired MRI and PET data
310   from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) were used for model development
311   and internal evaluation, while an independent Open Access Series of Imaging Studies 3 (OASIS-3)
312   cohort was used for external validation. The evaluation framework included three predefined compo-
313   nents: image-level fidelity assessment between synthetic and real PET, regional consistency analysis
314   of PET-derived biomarkers, and downstream clinical classification and mild cognitive impairment
315   stratification.


316   4.2    DIReCT++
317   DIReCT++ combines a frozen BiomedCLIP text encoder with a conditional 3D rectified flow model
318   to synthesize FDG- or AV45-PET from T1-weighted MRI and tracer/patient text guidance (Fig. 1).
319   The affine-adapter formulation aligns tracer-specific text features with PET features while regulating
320   cross-tracer representational similarity. Adapter parameters are optimized separately from the frozen
321   encoder and generator. We distinguish this formulation and its representation-level analyses from



                                                        14
322   generator configurations that use raw frozen tracer embeddings. The following subsections specify
323   the adaptation and conditioning procedures.

324   4.2.1   Domain-Adapted VLM

325   To incorporate domain-specific knowledge into the generation process, we develop an adaptive guid-
326   ance mechanism that leverages a frozen, pre-trained BiomedCLIP model. Specifically, we learn two
327   affine transformations, Adaptf and Adapta , to align the textual embeddings of FDG and AV-45 PET
328   modalities with their respective image feature spaces. Let cf , ca denote the BiomedCLIP textual
329   embeddings for FDG and AV-45, respectively. The frozen BiomedCLIP text and image encoders,
330   including their projection layers, produce 512-dimensional features. For the affine-adapter proce-
331   dure, each preprocessed 3D PET volume is represented by 160 two-dimensional slices along its third
332   array dimension. Each slice is intensity-normalized, resized to 224 × 224 pixels and converted to a
333   three-channel image before encoding; the slice features are averaged into a subject-level PET repre-
334   sentation. The same extraction procedure is used for both tracers in the revised adapter analysis.
335   For simplicity, we adopt lightweight affine transformations parameterized by a scalar scale and a
336   vector bias:

402   This study leveraged neuroimaging data from 1,857 participants enrolled in the Alzheimer’s Disease
403   Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), spanning cohorts from ADNI-1 through ADNI-4. All participants

408   matching each participant, which should not be interpreted as a nearest-date MRI–PET pairing

412         Participants were stratified into five clinically defined diagnostic categories to reflect the full

413   continuum of Alzheimer’s disease pathology: Cognitively Normal (CN; n = 577), Alzheimer’s disease
414   dementia (AD; n = 317), and three subtypes of Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). To account for
415   the heterogeneity of MCI, we further subclassified individuals based on delayed recall performance
416   on the WMS-R Logical Memory II Story A task: Early MCI (EMCI; n = 291) denoted a milder
417   impairment stage, whereas Late MCI (LMCI; n = 152) denoted a more severe impairment stage.
418   The remaining MCI participants not classified as EMCI or LMCI were grouped as MCI-unclassified
419   (MCI-U; n = 520). These categories define cross-sectional diagnostic and MCI-stage comparisons,

421      In the prepared clinical inputs, mean Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores decreased
422   from 29.40 in CN to 20.84 in AD; other clinical scores also varied across diagnostic groups (Table S1).
423   Clinical information was selected by participant identity, taking the first record in the source CSV
424   order, rather than the nearest or earliest imaging visit. Missing MMSE, GDSCALE, Global CDR,
425   FAQ and NPI-Q scores were filled using diagnostic-group-conditioned rules. Of 9,285 score cells,
426   1,751 were observed and retained, while 7,534 were missing in those source records and had values
427   consistent with the recovered filling rules. The resulting score distributions include simulated inputs
428   and should not be interpreted as independent evidence of between-group clinical differences. Group-
429   wise missingness is reported in Table S4; no new imputation was performed for the revised analyses.
430   The prepared weight data retain unresolved source-value issues, including CN entries ranging from
431   0 to 227 kg.
432      External evaluation used the Open Access Series of Imaging Studies (OASIS-3), a longitudinal
433   neuroimaging and clinical cohort focused on normal aging and Alzheimer’s disease (https://www.
434   oasis-brains.org). The retained external cohort comprised 419 participants with AV45-PET
435   processed using the Pet Unified Pipeline (PUP): 334 CN and 85 AD, with no MCI participants
436   (Table S2). FDG-PET from the smaller subset (n = 117) was excluded because it lacked the PUP
437   processing used for this external cohort. Subsequent model inputs were min–max normalized; the
438   external evaluation does not establish clinical SUV/SUVR calibration. The CN and AD groups
439   differed in age distribution. These data therefore evaluate external AV45 synthesis in this CN/AD
440   cohort, without establishing external generalizability for FDG synthesis or MCI-stage discrimination.
441   Complete run-specific records of the external clinical prompt fields and missing-field handling were
442   not retained, limiting reproducibility of that historical evaluation.
443      To ensure precise spatial correspondence between MRI and dual-modality PET images, we im-
444   plemented a standardized preprocessing pipeline. T1-weighted MRI scans were first skull-stripped
445   using FreeSurfer [31] and subjected to histogram matching for contrast normalization. Each PET
446   image was then rigidly registered to its corresponding MRI. Both modalities were jointly affinely
447   normalized to the MNI152 standard space using SPM [32, 33], with identical transformation ma-
448   trices applied to preserve cross-modality alignment. Following spatial normalization, all volumes
449   were cropped to a common field of view of 160 × 192 × 160 voxels (1 mm3 isotropic resolution) and
450   intensity-normalized to the [0, 1] range via min–max scaling. For quantitative regional analysis,
451   T1-weighted MRI scans were parcellated into 98 anatomical regions using SynthSeg [27], and the
452   resulting ROI masks were applied to the spatially aligned PET images. ADNI provided preprocessed
453   PET images upstream of this pipeline. Spatial registration and intensity normalization do not es-


                                                        18
454   tablish comprehensive scanner-specific harmonization; acquisition and upstream processing details
455   could not be verified for every actual model input.
456      PET synthesis used subject-level two-fold cross-validation with fixed, approximately equal parti-
457   tions (929 and 928 participants; split seed 42). In each fold, training and held-out participants were
458   disjoint. Held-out predictions from the two folds were combined into an out-of-fold (OOF) dataset
459   for generation analyses, with 1,598 valid FDG and 1,365 valid AV45 observations before the sepa-
460   rately specified MRI/mask QC exclusion used in masked evaluation. Downstream classification used
461   a separate task-specific stratified three-fold protocol, described below. The classification analysis re-
462   tained the existing author-provided synthetic PET dataset under the intended two-fold OOF design;
463   its image-to-checkpoint correspondence to the reconstructed Full generation experiments could not
464   be independently established. Classification results are therefore reported as a separate input-asset
465   analysis, rather than a downstream test of those reconstructed generator comparisons. Fixed splits,
466   seeds and implementation details are summarized in Table S5.


467   4.4      Experimental Setup and Evaluation
468   4.4.1     Competing Methods

469   To benchmark DIReCT++, six historical baseline methods were adapted to PET synthesis as de-
470   scribed below. Reconstructed comparisons additionally used Clinical Regression 3D U-Net, MRI-
471   only Swin-UNETR, TracerOnly, TabularMLP and separately trained Single models. Their input
472   conditions, training endpoints and implementation differences are retained; the comparisons are not
473   treated as uniformly optimized, equal-budget or single-component ablations.

474     1. CycleGAN [21]: A widely adopted unsupervised image-to-image translation framework that
475           enforces cycle-consistency to learn bidirectional mappings between unpaired domains. We
476           include it as a representative of non-probabilistic, GAN-based cross-modality synthesis.

477     2. Swin-UNet [22]: A hybrid architecture combining Swin Transformer blocks with a U-Net
478           encoder–decoder structure, excelling in high-resolution medical image generation and fine-
479           grained anatomical modeling.

480     3. SegGuidedDiff [23]: A segmentation-guided diffusion model adapted for 2D slice-based PET
481           synthesis using anatomical segmentation maps as guidance.

482     4. 3D DDIM [24, 25]: A deterministic fast-sampling variant of 3D diffusion models, imple-
483           mented using a 3D diffusion-model U-Net and a DDIM scheduler for the historical synthesis
484           comparison.

485     5. 3D Rectified Flow (3D RF) [26]: A flow-based generative model that shares DIReCT++’s
486           underlying ODE framework but lacks domain-informed conditioning.

487     6. DIReCT (Single-Modality Variant) [15]: Separate tracer-specific models synthesize one
488           PET tracer per forward pass, while DIReCT++ uses a shared network conditioned on the



                                                         19
489           target tracer. The comparison includes other implementation differences and does not isolate
490           joint-training synergy.

491      The 3D Swin-UNETR was adapted to PET intensity regression with a continuous single-channel
492   output and MSE loss, using MRI alone. Clinical Regression 3D U-Net used MRI and the same eight
493   clinical variables with target-tracer information, providing a clinically matched framework com-
494   parison. TracerOnly excluded patient-specific conditioning while retaining target-tracer guidance;
495   TabularMLP replaced the clinical text representation with a structured-variable encoder. Separate
496   Single models were trained per tracer. The principal comparison endpoint was epoch 165, except the
497   prespecified Swin-UNETR endpoint of epoch 200. These independently initialized training runs were
498   not designed to estimate variability over training seeds or to isolate every module as a single-factor
499   causal ablation.


500   4.5       Statistical Analysis
501   4.5.1     Image-Quality Evaluation

502   Whole-volume metrics include background; masked metrics use the frozen SynthSeg nonzero-label
503   union, which includes ventricles and CSF and is not a pure brain-parenchymal mask. MSE/MAE
504   average squared/absolute errors within that mask, and PSNR uses data range 1. Masked 3D SSIM
505   uses a Gaussian 7×7×7 kernel (σ = 1.5) and averages only centers whose complete window lies inside
506   the mask; outside-mask voxels were not set to zero and included in a whole-image SSIM average. The
507   masked comparison uses identical target and mask arrays across models. The existing MRI/mask
508   QC exclusion removes one subject per tracer, yielding FDG n = 1, 597 and AV45 n = 1, 364; this
509   exclusion is specific to the masked analysis and case selection and is not imposed on the classification
510   cohort.
511      Metrics are reported as across-subject mean ± sample s.d. Paired two-sided t-tests and 10,000
512   paired bootstrap resamples stratified by held-out fold (seed 42) assess the frozen generation predic-
513   tions. For each model comparison, Holm correction covers eight tests (two tracers × four metrics);
514   bootstrap 95% percentile CIs are unadjusted. Whole-volume and masked results use their respective
515   subject sets and target protocols. These intervals do not estimate retraining or split variability. Sup-
516   plemental cases are selected within each tracer’s QC-passed cohort by saved whole-volume SSIM:
517   nearest subject to the median or 10th percentile (linear quantiles), or the minimum for the worst
518   case; ties are resolved by stable participant-ID ordering, without visual selection.

519   4.5.2     Regional Group-Wise Comparison

520   Regional analysis used 98 SynthSeg ROIs defined on T1-weighted MRI. Representative regions
521   shown in Fig. 4C,D were selected for disease-related comparisons. FDG regions included the bilateral
522   hippocampus, isthmus cingulate cortex and supramarginal gyrus (atlas labels Left-Hippocampus,
523   Right-Hippocampus, and the ctx-lh-/ctx-rh- labels with suffixes isthmuscingulate and
524   supramarginal) [34–36]. AV45 regions included bilateral precuneus and inferior, middle, superior



                                                        20
525   and transverse temporal gyri (the corresponding ctx-lh-/ctx-rh- labels) [37]. Atlas-defined names
526   are used rather than the ambiguous temporoparietal-junction label.
527       The validation comprised two complementary analyses:

528      1. Intra-group consistency: We performed paired two-sided t-tests to compare normalized
529           regional uptake between real and Syn-PET images within each ROI, both across the overall
530           cohorts and within individual diagnostic groups. The null hypothesis was that the mean paired
531           difference in normalized regional uptake was zero. Mean paired differences with 95% confi-
532           dence intervals, mean absolute error (MAE), Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC),
533           the two-way absolute-agreement single-measure intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC(A,1)),
534           and Bland–Altman bias and 95% limits of agreement were also calculated to assess regional
535           agreement. For the 16 representative tracer–ROI combinations shown in Fig. 4A, B, we addi-
536           tionally performed subject-level ordinary least-squares regression of synthetic on real normal-
537           ized regional uptake in the overall cohorts, reporting slopes, intercepts, and their unadjusted
538           95% confidence intervals.

539      2. Inter-group discriminability: To assess whether Syn-PET preserves diagnostic signal, we
540           conducted independent two-sided Student’s t-tests (assuming equal variances) comparing mean
541           normalized regional uptake between the AD and CN groups in the same ROIs—first using real
542           PET and then repeating the analysis with Syn-PET. Cohen’s d was calculated separately
543           for real and synthetic PET as the AD–CN mean difference divided by the pooled within-
544           group sample standard deviation, to characterize the direction and magnitude of the group
545           differences.

546       All t-tests were two-tailed, and p-values were adjusted for multiple ROI comparisons using the
547   Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure [38]. For the paired real–synthetic com-
548   parisons, FDR correction was applied across 98 ROIs separately for each tracer and analysis group
549   (overall, AD, CN, EMCI, LMCI, and MCI), yielding 12 families (2 tracers × 6 analysis groups)
550   and 1,176 tests in total. For the AD–CN comparisons, FDR correction was applied across 98 ROIs
551   separately for each tracer and PET type (real or synthetic), yielding four families and 392 tests in
552   total. Statistical significance was defined as an FDR-adjusted q < 0.05.

553   4.5.3      Diagnostic Classification and MCI Stage Discrimination

554   Image-based classifiers used a DenseNet121 backbone [20] to evaluate three cross-sectional tasks:
555   AD/CN; EMCI+LMCI/CN; and EMCI/LMCI. Positive classes were AD, EMCI+LMCI and LMCI,
556   respectively. The task-specific cohorts contained 470, 741 and 436 participants, excluding MCI-
557   U. Seven imaging conditions used MRI alone or MRI combined with real/synthetic FDG, AV45
558   or both tracers. Two further conditions were Clinical-only (an 18-parameter linear classifier) and
559   MRI+Clinical. Both clinical baselines used the same eight clinical variables as the conditioning
560   inputs; their information content is diagnosis-related and affected by historical group-conditioned
561   filling.



                                                         21
562      All conditions used the same task-specific subject-level stratified three-fold outer splits (seed 42).
563   Within each outer training set, a fixed stratified 15% validation subset selected the checkpoint by
564   validation ACC; the outer test was evaluated once after reloading that checkpoint. Imaging models
565   used Adam (learning rate 10−4 ), batch size 5, up to 200 epochs and validation every two epochs.
566   Clinical and combined-model settings are retained separately in the reproducibility records rather
567   than assumed identical to every imaging model. Thresholded metrics use 0.5; AUC uses continuous
568   positive-class scores. We report ACC, AUC, SEN, SPE and balanced ACC as equally weighted
569   means across the three folds with sample s.d.
570      Bootstrap CIs use 5,000 paired resamples within each fold-by-class stratum (seed 20261102);
571   each condition shares participant multiplicities within a resample. Metrics are recalculated by fold
572   and averaged equally, rather than pooling differently trained model scores. Paired differences use
573   the same resamples. The 95% percentile intervals are marginal and unadjusted for simultaneous
574   coverage; they condition on the trained models, class proportions and fixed splits and do not estimate
575   retraining variability or eliminate dependence from overlapping training sets. DeLong tests compare
576   matched predictions separately within each outer fold. The 18 original imaging comparisons and 36
577   additional clinical comparisons form separate Holm families; raw p values are retained. No overall
578   three-fold DeLong p value is reported. ROC curves average fold-specific TPR over an FPR grid with
579   step 0.005; pointwise 95% bands use 2,000 fold-by-class resamples. The legend AUC is the mean
580   empirical fold AUC, not pooled AUC or an integral of the finite grid.
581      Similar observed Real/Syn performance or a non-significant difference does not establish biolog-
582   ical fidelity, equivalence or clinical interchangeability. Clinical-only and MRI+Clinical comparisons
583   assess whether the reported synthetic-image classifier adds information beyond the provided clinical
584   fields; they do not resolve label circularity introduced by diagnostic-group-conditioned simulated
585   inputs.
586      Demographic sensitivity analyses separately stratified recorded age at 75 years and sex (F/M),
587   without crossing the strata or changing thresholds/checkpoints. Each task, fold, subgroup and true-
588   class stratum was resampled 5,000 times (seed 20261103); seven imaging inputs shared resample
589   multiplicities, and fold metrics were averaged equally. These unadjusted marginal subgroup CIs
590   were not used to infer interactions. Recorded ages could not be verified as MRI-visit ages because
591   the available demographic export lacked visit dates. Age and sex were not mutually adjusted, and
592   clinical-score or residual within-group confounding was not eliminated.


593   4.6       Implementation Details
594   The reconstructed generator is a four-level 3D U-Net with channels (32, 64, 64, 128), one residual
595   block per level, cross-attention only at the deepest level, and conditioning dimension 512. It con-
596   tains 9,925,921 trainable parameters, excluding the frozen BiomedCLIP encoders. Generator train-
597   ing used Adam (learning rate 2.5 × 10−5 ), batch size 1 and AMP. The principal generation endpoint
598   is epoch 165; documented epoch-200 outputs and the MRI-only Swin-UNETR endpoint are distin-
599   guished from that endpoint. Recorded training/split settings by experiment family are summarized
600   in Table S5; hardware and software differed between historical, reconstructed and timing runs. The



                                                        22
601   historical estimate of 138 GPU-hours was not independently verified and is not used to claim full
602   convergence or a speedup over other methods.
603      Inference timing used the Full fold-1 epoch-165 generator on an RTX 4090 (24 GB), PyTorch
604   2.5.1+cu121, CUDA FP32 and GPU-resident 1 × 1 × 160 × 192 × 160 MRI with cached text context.
605   After ten warm-up iterations, 50 synchronized repetitions per tracer gave FDG 251.015 ± 0.187 ms
606   and AV45 251.411 ± 0.256 ms (mean ± sample s.d. across repeated measurements of one fixed
607   input). Peak PyTorch-allocated memory was 8.29 GiB and reserved memory 13.27 GiB, excluding
608   other processes. These measurements exclude text encoding, preprocessing, transfers and file I/O;
609   they describe a single-tracer forward pass, not end-to-end clinical workflow latency. Validated full-
610   network FLOP counts and identical-protocol baseline timing were unavailable, so no FLOP estimate
611   or baseline speedup ratio is claimed.
